Future directions and conclusion

Where Could This Research Go Next?

Vision-guided haptic systems are moving from simple object alerts toward more detailed forms of spatial, physical, and context-aware feedback. Future systems may adapt their signals to the environment, the user, and the task being performed.

What this tutorial demonstrated

Computer vision provides information about objects, distance, direction, motion, and environmental structure. That information is then processed by an embedded system and translated into haptic parameters such as motor location, vibration intensity, and pulse frequency.

The reviewed systems show that vision-to-haptic feedback can support visually impaired navigation, robot teleoperation, and virtual-reality interaction. However, each application uses different visual inputs and different types of haptic output.

Future research directions

  • Personalized vibration mappings based on individual user preferences.
  • Adaptive feedback that changes according to the environment and task.
  • Improved depth estimation and camera-sensor fusion.
  • Lower-latency processing on wearable embedded hardware.
  • More comfortable, lightweight, and energy-efficient actuators.
  • User testing in crowded, outdoor, and unpredictable environments.

From fixed rules to generated feedback

Many current systems use manually designed rules. For example, a close obstacle on the left may activate a strong left-side vibration. Future systems may use multimodal models to consider visual information, language instructions, object relationships, and user intent together.

The VLH system is an example of this direction. It combines visual input and language commands to produce both robot actions and haptic signals. Its application is aerial robotics and virtual reality rather than assistive navigation, but its architecture demonstrates how haptic output could become a context-aware result of visual reasoning [5].

Conclusion

Computer vision alone does not provide a complete replacement for peripheral vision. It produces estimates that must be selected, prioritized, and translated into signals that a person can understand.

The most effective vision-to-haptic systems will likely combine reliable environmental sensing with carefully designed feedback patterns and human-centered evaluation. Their success should be measured not only by detection accuracy, but also by whether users can interpret the feedback quickly and navigate more safely.
